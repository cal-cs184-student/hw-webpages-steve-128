Overview

In this homework, I implemented a cloth simulation system and a series of GPU shader programs to model both physical behavior and realistic rendering effects. I first built a mass-spring system to simulate cloth dynamics, including external forces, numerical integration, and collisions with objects and with itself. It is fascinating to observe how different parameters, such as ks, density, and damping, can lead to a wide variety of cloth behaviors. I then extended the project by implementing several shading techniques, including diffuse and Blinn-Phong shading, texture mapping, bump mapping, displacement mapping, and mirror reflection using environment maps. One of the most interesting aspects of this assignment was observing how different techniques influence visual realism. Overall, this assignment helped me better understand both the physics behind cloth simulation and how vertex and fragment shaders work together to produce a wide range of realistic visual effects in real time.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

Implementation of collision with spheres and planes:

For sphere collisions, I checked whether a point mass was inside the sphere by comparing its distance from the sphere center to the sphere radius. The point mass is inside the sphere if its distance from the sphere center is less than the sphere radius. If it was inside, I projected the point mass outward along the direction from the sphere center to the point mass to find the tangent point on the sphere surface. Then, I computed a correction from last_position to that tangent point and applied a friction-scaled version of that correction to update the point mass’s position.

For plane collisions, I checked whether a point mass crossed from one side of the plane to the other during the last timestep by comparing the signs of the point-plane dot products at last_position and position. If the signs were different, then a crossing occurred. If a crossing occurred, I computed the line-plane intersection point, offset it slightly by SURFACE_OFFSET toward the side the point mass came from. Then, I moved the point mass to a friction-scaled corrected position.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

Implementation of handling self-collisions:

To handle self-collisions, I first build a spatial hash map using build_spatial_map(). This function places each point mass into a 3D grid cell based on its position, so that collision checks can be limited to nearby candidates instead of testing every pair of point masses. Following the spec, I use cell dimensions w, h, and t, compute the floored cell coordinates from the point mass position, and combine those coordinates into a single hash value using large constants to reduce hash collisions.

After building the map, I call self_collide() on each point mass. This function checks the other point masses stored in the same hash bucket and detects whether any are closer than 2 * thickness. If so, it computes a correction vector to push the current point mass away from the other one. The final position correction is the average of all such corrections, scaled down by simulation_steps to keep the response stable. This allows the cloth to collide with itself more realistically as it folds and falls, instead of passing through itself.

Part 5: Shaders

Shader program and vertex & fragment shaders:

A shader program is a small program that runs on the GPU to control how objects are drawn. The vertex shader runs once per vertex and is mainly responsible for transforming vertex positions from model space to screen space. It can also pass useful information, such as world-space position, normal vectors, tangent vectors, and UV coordinates, to the fragment shader. The fragment shader runs once per fragment, or potential pixel. It uses the interpolated values from the vertex shader to decide the final color of that pixel, which can include effects like lighting, texture mapping, bump mapping, and reflection. Together, the vertex shader determines where the geometry appears on screen, while the fragment shader determines how the surface looks.

Blinn-Phong shader:

Blinn-Phong shading combines three lighting components: ambient, diffuse, and specular. The ambient term gives the object a small amount of base light everywhere, so surfaces not directly facing the light are not completely black. The diffuse term models matte lighting. It depends on the angle between the surface normal and the light direction, so surfaces facing the light are brighter, while those angled away are darker. The specular term models shiny highlights. Instead of comparing the reflected light direction directly with the view direction, Blinn-Phong uses a halfway vector between the light direction and the view direction. If the normal is close to this halfway vector, the surface gets a bright highlight. Those three components are combined to create a more realistic lighting effect that captures both the overall illumination and the shiny highlights on surfaces. Ultimately, the Blinn-Phong shader creates a more relistic rendering of objects with the different components.

Screenshot of bump mapping and displacement mapping:

Bump mapping only modifies the surface normals to simulate small-scale detail, so the geometry itself remains unchanged. On the other hand, displacement mapping actually modifies the vertex positions based on the height map, producing real geometric changes that affect the shape and outline of the object. In the higher resolution mesh (-o 128 -a 128), the displacement becomes more pronounced and appears more chunky or exaggerated because more vertices are available to follow the height map. This shows that displacement mapping is highly sensitive to mesh resolution, while bump mapping produces more consistent visual results regardless of geometry density.
