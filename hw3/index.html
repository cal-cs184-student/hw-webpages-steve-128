Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Ray Generation:

For ray generation, I first implemented the generate_ray(x, y) function, which constructs a camera ray in world space from normalized image coordinates. Inside this function, the normalized coordinates are mapped onto the sensor plane using the horizontal and vertical field of view of the camera. In camera space, the ray starts at the camera origin and points toward the corresponding point on the sensor plane, which is placed one unit in front of the camera origin. The ray direction is then transformed from camera space into world space using the camera-to-world rotation matrix, while the ray origin is set to the camera’s world-space position. Finally, the direction is normalized, and the ray’s min_t and max_t values are initialized using the near and far clipping planes.

After that, I implemented raytrace_pixel, which is responsible for generating multiple camera rays for each pixel and averaging their results. For each sample, I first take the pixel coordinates and add a sub-pixel sample offset, then divide by the image width and height to convert them into normalized coordinates in the range [0,1] x [0,1]. These normalized coordinates are passed into generate_ray to produce a ray through that sampled location on the pixel. Next, I call est_radiance_global_illumination on each ray, accumulate the returned radiance values, and divide by the total number of samples to compute the average pixel color. Finally, this averaged color is then written into the sample buffer.

Primitive Intersection:

After a ray is generated, it is tested against the primitives in the scene, such as triangles and spheres. Each primitive checks whether the ray intersects it within the valid range of t, which is restricted by the ray’s min_t and max_t. If an intersection exists and lies within this interval, the primitive reports a hit. In the case of a full intersection test, the primitive also updates the intersection record with information about the closest hit point. This includes the t-value of the hit, the surface normal at the intersection, a pointer to the intersected primitive, and a pointer to its Bidirectional Scattering Distribution Function(BSDF). In addition, the ray’s max_t is updated to the new closest intersection distance so that any later intersections farther away can be ignored. This ensures that the renderer always keeps the nearest visible surface along the ray.

Triangle Intersection Algorithm:

For the triangle intersection algorithm, I implemented the Moller-Trumbore algorithm, which is an efficient method for determining if a ray intersects a triangle. To implement this algorithm, I first compute the two edges of the triangle. Using those edges together with the ray direction, I computed a determinant value to test whether the ray is parallel to the triangle. If the determinant is close to zero, the ray does not intersect the triangle. Otherwise, I continued by solving for the barycentric coordinates, which determine whether the intersection point lies inside the triangle. If either barycentric coordinate is negative, or if their sum is greater than one, then the point lies outside the triangle and the intersection is rejected. Once the barycentric coordinates are valid, I computed the corresponding t-value along the ray. If that t lies within the ray’s valid interval, then the intersection is accepted and the ray’s max_t is updated to that value. For the full intersection function, I also used the barycentric coordinates to interpolate the three vertex normals of the triangle. This interpolated normal was then normalized and stored in the intersection record along with the hit distance, primitive pointer, and BSDF pointer.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

BVH Construction Algorithm:

To implement the construct_bvh function, I first compute the bounding box of all primitives in the current range and create a BVHNode containing that box. If the number of primitives in the range is less than or equal to max_leaf_size, then I set the node’s primitive range (start and end) to cover all primitives in that range and return the node as a leaf. Otherwise, I compute a centroid bounding box by expanding over the centroids of each primitive’s bounding box. I choose the split axis as the axis with the largest extent in this centroid bounding box because it has the most spread out primitives along that dimension, which can lead to better partitioning. In fact, the split point is just the midpoint of the centroid bounding box along the chosen axis, which is efficient to compute and often results in a good balance between the two subsets. Then, I split at the midpoint of the centroid bounding box along the chosen axis and partition the primitives into left and right subsets. To prevent infinite recursion case where all the primitives lies on one side of the split point, I check if the mid point is the same as the start or the end. If so, I manually reset the mid point to be the start + (end - start) / 2, which ensures that the primitives are split into two non-empty subsets. Finally, I recursively call construct_bvh on the left and right subsets to build the child nodes, and set the current node’s left and right pointers to those child nodes before returning it.

Rendering Time Comparison:

To render CBlucy.dae with 1 thread at a resolution of 800x600, it takes 0.1013 seconds with BVH acceleration enabled, while rendering the same image without BVH takes 2381.629 seconds. This corresponds to a speedup of about 23,500 times. For cow.dae under the same settings, rendering takes 0.155 seconds with BVH and 28.2891 seconds without BVH, which is a speedup of about 182 times. These results show that BVH acceleration dramatically improves rendering performance, especially for scenes with large numbers of primitives. Without BVH, every ray must be tested against every primitive in the scene, causing rendering time to increase rapidly as scene complexity grows. With BVH, rays are first tested against bounding boxes, allowing the renderer to quickly discard large groups of primitives that cannot be intersected. This greatly reduces the number of expensive primitive intersection tests. The speedup is much more significant for CBlucy.dae than for cow.dae, which suggests that CBlucy.dae has a far more complex geometry and therefore benefits more from hierarchical spatial partitioning. Overall, the comparison shows that BVH is essential for making ray tracing practical on highly complex scenes.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

Uniform hemisphere sampling:

In estimate_direct_lighting_hemisphere, I estimate direct illumination by sampling incoming directions uniformly over the hemisphere above the surface point. Initially, for num_samples, I sample a direction wi from the hemisphereSampler and transform it to the world coordinate system by multiplying it by the o2w matrix. Then, I created a ray from the hit point along that direction. When creating this ray, I set min_t = EPS_F to avoid numerical precision issues that would cause the ray to immediately intersect the same surface it originated from. If the ray hits an emissive object, I treat its emission as the incoming radiance L_i. I then accumulate the Monte Carlo estimator f(w_out, wi) * L_i * abs_cos_theta(wi) / pdf, where the pdf for uniform hemisphere sampling is 1 / (2π). After summing all samples, I divide by the total number of samples.

Light importance sampling:

In estimate_direct_lighting_importance, I estimate direct illumination by sampling directly from each light source instead of sampling arbitrary hemisphere directions. Initially, for each light in the scene, I choose the number of samples to be 1 if the light is a point light source else ns_area_light. Then, for each num_samples I call SceneLight::sample_L to get the emitted radiance as a Vector3D, write the world-space incident direction, the distance to the light, and the light pdf. Next,I transform wi_world into local space and skip the sample if it lies below the surface. Then, similar to the uniform hemisphere sampling method, I create a ray from the hit point along the sampled direction and check if it hits an emissive object. Again, the ray uses min_t = EPS_F and max_t = distToLight - EPS_F so that I only test whether something blocks the light before the ray reaches it. If the shadow ray is not blocked, I accumulate the Monte Carlo estimator with f(w_out, wi) * L_i * abs_cos_theta(wi) / pdf. Finally, I divide the accumulated radiance by the total number of samples taken across all lights to get the final estimate of direct lighting.

Uniform Hemisphere Sampling V.S. Light Importance Sampling:

Uniform hemisphere sampling and light sampling both provide unbiased estimates of direct illumination, but their variance is very different. With uniform hemisphere sampling, directions are chosen randomly over the entire upper hemisphere, so most samples do not hit a light source and contribute little or nothing. This results in very high variance and noisy images, especially when the number of samples is low. On the other hand, light importance sampling directly samples directions toward the light sources, so a much larger fraction of samples contribute meaningful illumination. As a result, light sampling converges much faster and produces significantly cleaner images for the same sample budget. This is especially evident in the scene that had 1 light ray and 1 sample per pixel, where the uniform hemisphere sampling image is extremely noisy and we can only see the general shape of the object while the light importance sampling image is much clearer and we can basically see some level of detail of the object.
