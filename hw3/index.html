Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Ray Generation:

For ray generation, I first implemented the generate_ray(x, y) function, which constructs a camera ray in world space from normalized image coordinates. Inside this function, the normalized coordinates are mapped onto the sensor plane using the horizontal and vertical field of view of the camera. In camera space, the ray starts at the camera origin and points toward the corresponding point on the sensor plane, which is placed one unit in front of the camera origin. The ray direction is then transformed from camera space into world space using the camera-to-world rotation matrix, while the ray origin is set to the camera’s world-space position. Finally, the direction is normalized, and the ray’s min_t and max_t values are initialized using the near and far clipping planes.

After that, I implemented raytrace_pixel, which is responsible for generating multiple camera rays for each pixel and averaging their results. For each sample, I first take the pixel coordinates and add a sub-pixel sample offset, then divide by the image width and height to convert them into normalized coordinates in the range [0,1] x [0,1]. These normalized coordinates are passed into generate_ray to produce a ray through that sampled location on the pixel. Next, I call est_radiance_global_illumination on each ray, accumulate the returned radiance values, and divide by the total number of samples to compute the average pixel color. Finally, this averaged color is then written into the sample buffer.

Primitive Intersection:

After a ray is generated, it is tested against the primitives in the scene, such as triangles and spheres. Each primitive checks whether the ray intersects it within the valid range of t, which is restricted by the ray’s min_t and max_t. If an intersection exists and lies within this interval, the primitive reports a hit. In the case of a full intersection test, the primitive also updates the intersection record with information about the closest hit point. This includes the t-value of the hit, the surface normal at the intersection, a pointer to the intersected primitive, and a pointer to its Bidirectional Scattering Distribution Function(BSDF). In addition, the ray’s max_t is updated to the new closest intersection distance so that any later intersections farther away can be ignored. This ensures that the renderer always keeps the nearest visible surface along the ray.

Triangle Intersection Algorithm:

For the triangle intersection algorithm, I implemented the Moller-Trumbore algorithm, which is an efficient method for determining if a ray intersects a triangle. To implement this algorithm, I first compute the two edges of the triangle. Using those edges together with the ray direction, I computed a determinant value to test whether the ray is parallel to the triangle. If the determinant is close to zero, the ray does not intersect the triangle. Otherwise, I continued by solving for the barycentric coordinates, which determine whether the intersection point lies inside the triangle. If either barycentric coordinate is negative, or if their sum is greater than one, then the point lies outside the triangle and the intersection is rejected. Once the barycentric coordinates are valid, I computed the corresponding t-value along the ray. If that t lies within the ray’s valid interval, then the intersection is accepted and the ray’s max_t is updated to that value. For the full intersection function, I also used the barycentric coordinates to interpolate the three vertex normals of the triangle. This interpolated normal was then normalized and stored in the intersection record along with the hit distance, primitive pointer, and BSDF pointer.
